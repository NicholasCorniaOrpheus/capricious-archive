# STYLE PROFILE: the Italian Cinquecento–early Seicento lyric corpus (c. 1500–1640)

The corpus is a mixed anthology of printed and musical lyric. It includes Petrarchist sonnets by women poets (Gambara, Stampa, Colonna, Terracina, Battiferri, Andreini, Franco, Matraini), encomiastic sonnets for patrons, madrigal texts set by composers (Agostini, Wert, Viola, Marenzio, Vecchi, Passetto), ottava rima from epic and romance (Ariosto, Tasso, Aretino, Santinelli), and Latin or dialect oddities. The imitator should aim for the central Petrarchist voice, with occasional madrigalesque and encomiastic registers.

## 1. Topics and how they combine

The statistics show *praise* (84) far ahead of everything else, then *metaphor* (44), *pastoral* (26), *despair* and *oxymoron* (22 each), and *longing* (16). Most poems combine two of these.

- **Praise of a lady, patron or fellow poet.** The addressee is a "Donna reale," a "Dea," or a poet such as Flaminio Scala, Marino, Varchi or Venier. The praise comes with the *inexpressibility topos*: "Veggio mancare ingegni, e lingue, e carte." Its usual setting is a "novo Tempio," a monument built from verse.
- **Love as torment.** The speaker suffers in absence, abandonment or jealousy, and often asks for death: "Fammi più presto pregoti morire." Weeping (37), torment (35), flame (30) and chains (18) are the stock depictions.
- **Oxymoron and the "dolce" paradox.** "Oh dolce servitù, dolce tormento," "Nasce la vita mia dal mio morire," "Languendo godo e di morir disio." Anaphora (15) often carries the paradox: "Tu il fuoco pur, tu il ghiaccio, e tu i martiri."
- **Pastoral.** Nymphs and shepherds (Clori, Filli, Alessi, Nisa, Tirsi) sing beside streams, among "frondi e fiori," with the "aura" and the "augelletti" taking part. Pastoral usually turns erotic, as with the kiss in "Baciami, che i tuo' baci."
- **Sun, stars and light.** The lady is "mio bel Sole" or "vivo sole" (Sun 21, light beam 23, celestial body 23, star 17). Her eyes are "duo soli" and "lumi." Absence means darkness or winter.
- **Smaller strands.**
  - Alchemy and cosmology (Santinelli, Bruno, Maier): Chaos, the four Elements, Auro, Foco.
  - *Querelle des femmes*: women defended against slanderers.
  - Commedia dell'arte homage to Scala and the Andreini.
  - Religious or penitential sonnets (Colonna, Stampa's "Mesta, e pentita").
  - Elegy for a dead son or husband.
  - Chivalric epic (Ariosto, Tasso).

## 2. Lexicon and orthography

Use the printed conventions of the period, and expect some inconsistency.

- **Latinizing *h*:** *hor, hora, hoggi, honore, huomo, havea, haver, hai, ha, hebbe, humile, humor, herba, hemisfero.* Alongside these, *Ch'*, *c'*, and *che'l* appear.
- **Ampersand and *et*:** "& " is common, as are *et* before vowels and *e'n, e'l, d'*.
- **Elisions and apocopes written in:** *ch'io, s'io, quest'è, ognor, ogn'hor, ogn'un, fior', dolc'accenti, bell'avorio, vo', tien, ponno, fur, furo, sete.*
- **Capitalized abstractions:** *Amore, Fortuna, Morte, Cielo, Mondo, Sol, Natura, Tempio, Elementi.* Addressee names are sometimes set in capitals (*LAURA, FLAMMA, BARBARA*), often with a pun on the name.
- **Poetic archaisms:** *lassa, ohimè, deh, oh, aita, speme, disio/desio, martire, doglia, pianto, sospir, cor, alma, lume, ciglio, fera, stral, face, facella, foco, gielo, scorno, ch'a, ond'.*
- **Fixed vocabulary:** *crudel, empio, fiero, aspro, rio, dolce, soave, chiaro, vago, leggiadro, gentil.* Epithets come in doubles or triples: "tanto, e sì lieve," "dolci, acerbi, e fieri."
- **Classical names:** Phebo, Apollo, Cinthia, Venere, Ciprigna, Marte, Giove, Elicona, Parnaso, Battro e Tile.
- **Dialect and macaronic material is rare** (Calmo's Venetian *Sapie cari signori*, *zovena*, *Daspuò che no ve vezo*). Use it only in comic *commedia* pieces.
- **Latin** appears in the elegiac distich and hexameter pieces (Folengo, Bruno, Cardano, Kircher).

## 3. Forms, metres, rhyme

Poetic types are sonnet 123, madrigal 98, ottava rima 70, barzelletta 10, terzina 8, canzone 6, eclogue 5, with one each of ode, sestina and humorous poem. Metre is hendecasyllable 316, heptasyllable 107, octosyllable 8, hexameter 4. Most poems are therefore hendecasyllabic, with a heptasyllabic minority.

- **Sonnet:** ABBA ABBA CDE CDE or CDC DCD, and sometimes ABBA ABBA CDC CDC. The sestet occasionally takes a final couplet (…EE). The structure is quatrains for the situation, and tercets for the turn, the resolution or the *pointe*. Examples: "Ma io, s'avvien che perda il mio bel Sole… / Non spero haver, né voglio alcun conforto."
- **Madrigal:** a single stanza of 6–13 lines, free mix of 7- and 11-syllable lines, with an epigrammatic or paradoxical close, usually a rhymed couplet. Typical rhyme: ABbCDdEE or AbBaCC. It is usually one sentence of address, as in "Dicesti anima mia / Ch'ardi per me d'Amore."
- **Ottava rima:** ABABABCC in hendecasyllables. It appears in the epic/chivalric tradition ("Le donne, i cavallier…") and in many one-stanza love or encomiastic poems. The couplet gives the *sentenza*: "Ma non può far già che io non sia beato / Se tutti i Mondi non cangiasser stato."
- **Barzelletta, canzonetta, canzone:** short strophes of 3–6 lines in 5-, 6- or 7-syllable lines, with refrain or repetition: "Quest'amore, / Quest'arsura…"; "Chi vuol veder un bosco…". These are the anaphoric and list-based songs.
- **Musical texts:** half-lines or fragments joined by enjambment, with unusual line lengths and *rime al mezzo* ("Hor non finge, / Horr non pinge").

## 4. Imagery and rhetorical devices

- **Conceits of fire and water:** "Se con l'umor m'ardete? … Ove il foco sommerga, e l'acqua incenda." Ice and fire sit together: "Fiorir il Ghiaccio & fiammeggiar la Neve."
- **Amor the archer and jailer:** "strali, catene, foco"; "La rete fu di queste fila d'oro"; the heart as prey, with the lady as wild beast or "fera." A stock comparison is the moth in the flame, the stag at the stream.
- **Light:** the lady as Sun, whose absence brings night, winter or eclipse. Nature is out of order without her ("Veggio torbide l'acque, e l'aere nero").
- **Singing and music:** the lady's song stops winds, rivers and the Sun ("Fermano il corso ai venti"). Cygnets, Orpheus, Philomena and Sirens appear.
- **Navigation:** the storm, shipwreck and rocks ("scoglio") stand for love or life.
- **Mythological comparison:** the lady surpasses Venus, Minerva or Diana, and the speaker is Icarus, Tantalus or Argus.
- **Devices to use constantly:**
  - antithesis and oxymoron;
  - anaphora with lists ("Tu…, tu…"; "Oh…, oh…");
  - apostrophe ("Oh valle inferna," "Occhi lucenti, e belli");
  - rhetorical questions ("Ov'è…? Ov'è…?");
  - enumeration of three or more items ("ingegni, e lingue, e carte");
  - polyptoton and repetition ("dolce… dolcemente");
  - the *acrostic or name play*;
  - *adynata* ("Pria che…Senza foglie vedrò mirti et allori");
  - the *correlative* "Come… così…".

## 5. Typical openings and closings

Openings favour an apostrophe, an exclamation, or a "come/quando" clause. Closings favour a paradox or *sentenza*.

1. "Amor tu causi tutt'il penar mio" (apostrophe to Amore)
2. "Oh dolce servitù, dolce tormento" (exclamatory oxymoron)
3. "Ov'è (misera me) quell'aureo crine," (anaphoric question)
4. "Quando nell'ocean l'altera fronte / Inchina il Sole" (temporal or natural setting)
5. "Occhi lucenti, e belli," (address to eyes)
6. Closing, a command to the lady: "Baciami, che i tuo' baci / Fien de la lingua mia nodi tenaci."
7. Closing with a reversal: "Ch'in voi di DIO l'imagine s'adori."
